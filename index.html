Hotmail vs Outlook in 2026: One Inbox, Two Names, and a 30-Year Internet Mystery

Your email address can be a time capsule. Your inbox doesn't have to be.

A useful distinction
Two addresses with different endings are not automatically one account. They share a mailbox only if linked as aliases.

Imagine opening an old contacts list. One friend has @hotmail.com after their name. Another has @outlook.com. The Hotmail address feels like a souvenir from the era of dial-up connections and custom MSN Messenger statuses. The Outlook address sounds newer, maybe even more professional.

Here's the twist: both people can be using the same Microsoft email service today.

So is Hotmail different from Outlook? Is an older address better? And should someone with a perfectly good Hotmail account change it just because the branding changed years ago?

Let's untangle the naming mystery without the usual tech jargon.

The 30-second answer

Hotmail is the older name and email-address brand. Outlook.com is Microsoft's current web-based email service. Existing @hotmail.com addresses can still work through Outlook.com. An @outlook.com address doesn't automatically come with a better inbox, better security, or a higher chance of reaching someone else's inbox.

There's one extra complication: Outlook can also mean Microsoft's email app or its work-and-school email products. Same word, different things.

The rebrand that didn't erase your address

Hotmail launched on July 4, 1996, making it 30 years old in 2026. In 1997, Microsoft acquired the pioneering webmail service. Then, in 2012, it introduced Outlook.com as the new face of its consumer email service. By February 2013, Microsoft was officially moving Hotmail users to Outlook.com.

But it wasn't a mass eviction of old email addresses. Microsoft said Hotmail customers would keep their addresses, passwords, messages, and contacts through the transition.

That's why an address ending in @hotmail.com can still receive mail in 2026. The name printed on the address stayed; the service behind it moved on.

Think of it like a favorite café that changes its name and renovates the interior. You don't need to move house just because the sign over the door has changed.

The timeline in four dates:

  • 1996: Hotmail launches, helping bring email into the browser.
  • 1997: Microsoft acquires Hotmail.
  • 2012: Outlook.com launches in preview as Microsoft's new consumer email experience.
  • 2013: Outlook.com officially launches and the migration of Hotmail users begins.

The three things people accidentally call “Outlook”

This is where most comparisons go wrong. The word is doing three different jobs.

1. The address: @outlook.com

This is the part people see after the @ sign. It's an email-address domain, just like @hotmail.com. It tells other people where to send a message, not which app you use to read it.

2. The service: Outlook.com

This is Microsoft's consumer email service. Sign in at outlook.live.com and you can access a supported personal Microsoft mailbox, including an existing Hotmail address.

3. The app: Microsoft Outlook

This is the software you can use to read email and manage your calendar on a computer or phone. The Outlook app can support accounts with various email addresses. Microsoft also offers Outlook experiences for work and school, which are different from its free consumer Outlook.com service.

In short: the address is your nameplate, the service is the postal system, and the app is the mailbox you open. Once you separate those three, the Hotmail-versus-Outlook debate gets much easier.

Should you keep your old Hotmail address?

Usually, yes—if it still works and you like it.

An older email address may be connected to important accounts, years of conversations, online purchases, contacts, or recovery settings. Replacing it everywhere can create work without solving a real problem.

It makes sense to keep it if you can still sign in securely, receive messages, and recover the account if you lose access. A dated-looking domain is not an emergency.

If you want an address that looks cleaner for a résumé or portfolio, you can consider a new @outlook.com address. But choosing a simple name—something easy to spell and share—often matters more than the label after the @.

For a business with employees, a company-owned domain and properly managed business email are generally a stronger long-term choice than building operations around personal free accounts.

Can you change from Hotmail to Outlook without losing your inbox?

Sometimes, you can add an email alias to your existing Microsoft account rather than opening a completely separate account. Microsoft says aliases share a password, contacts, subscriptions, and account settings, and they can be used to send and receive mail.

That doesn't mean any two existing addresses can be merged. An address already attached to another Microsoft account cannot simply be claimed by your account. And deleting a Microsoft-domain alias can have lasting consequences, so read the current instructions carefully before changing anything.

The sensible approach is to first check Microsoft's official alias-management guide, confirm which account you are editing, and avoid removing an address you may still need.

What matters more than the word after the @?

The debate over Hotmail versus Outlook can distract from the things that genuinely determine whether an email account is useful.

Ownership and recovery

Can you sign in? Do you control your recovery methods? If you get locked out, do you have a legitimate path back in? Those questions matter a lot more than whether the domain looks old-fashioned.

Security habits

A unique password and Microsoft's available two-step verification are far more meaningful than switching from @hotmail.com to @outlook.com. Turn on extra sign-in protection and keep recovery information current.

Appropriate use

An account for family messages has different needs from an account used for client correspondence. For professional communication, clarity, consistency, and reliability matter. For legitimate email marketing, permission and good sending practices matter; an address ending is not a magic deliverability shortcut.

The real difference behind third-party listings

Some third-party marketplaces, including Buy Mail Store, list Hotmail and Outlook addresses as separate categories. That tells you how the listings are organized; it does not mean Microsoft operates two unrelated inbox platforms.

There is an important ownership consideration: Microsoft's Services Agreement says Microsoft account credentials cannot be transferred to another user or entity. If you encounter offers for pre-created email accounts, check those rules and consider access, recovery, and account-security risks. A label like “aged” does not guarantee trust, deliverability, or lasting access.

A better way to choose: three quick scenarios

“I already have Hotmail, and everything works.” Keep using it. Sign in through Outlook.com, secure the account, and don't change a working address purely for cosmetic reasons.

“I'm creating my first Microsoft email address.” An available @outlook.com address is a simple, modern option. Choose something memorable rather than hunting for an older domain.

“I need email for an actual business.” Consider a custom domain and a service designed for organizational account management. That choice matters more than the Hotmail/Outlook branding question.

Frequently asked questions

Is Hotmail still working in 2026?

Yes. Microsoft continues to explain how existing Hotmail users can sign in through Outlook.com. Your account still needs to be active and accessible.

Is Hotmail the same as Outlook?

For Microsoft's consumer webmail service today, Hotmail is the earlier brand and Outlook.com is its successor. But “Outlook” can also mean an email application or a work-and-school product, so context matters.

Can a Hotmail user email an Outlook user?

Absolutely. They are ordinary email addresses, and messages can be exchanged just like email between different providers.

Do @hotmail.com and @outlook.com automatically share an inbox?

No. Two separate addresses are not automatically one account. They may share a mailbox if they are configured as aliases on the same Microsoft account.

Can I create a brand-new Hotmail address?

Microsoft's current account-creation guidance directs new users to create a Microsoft account, generally with a current Outlook.com address. Existing Hotmail addresses are supported, but you shouldn't assume a new @hotmail.com address will be offered at sign-up.

Which is more professional: Hotmail or Outlook?

An @outlook.com address may look more current to some recipients, but neither suffix makes someone more qualified or trustworthy. A clear address and professional communication matter more.

Does an older email address automatically have better deliverability?

No. The age or ending of an address is not proof that messages will reach recipients' inboxes. Sending behavior, content, security, and recipient engagement all matter.

The bottom line: the name changed, not your need for a dependable inbox

Hotmail didn't vanish in the way people sometimes imagine. The brand stepped aside; many of its addresses stayed. Outlook.com became the modern consumer service, while “Outlook” also became an umbrella name people use for several email experiences.

So don't choose your next email address based on nostalgia—or the assumption that a newer name must be technically superior. Choose an address you control, can protect, and are comfortable sharing.

The internet changes its labels all the time. Your ability to receive an important message shouldn't depend on keeping up with every rebrand.
